Abstract

Robots that assemble products from instructions written for humans face two errors that often go unnoticed until a later part does not fit: the vision-language model (VLM) that reads the instructions can misread a step, and the robot can execute a step imperfectly. We ask where the robot should inspect its work and when it should ask a human operator. Experienced workers ask a supervisor when a drawing does not show which part goes where, check connectors before the next part covers them, and look closely before a tight insertion. We turn these behaviors into a hierarchical behavior tree (BT). Before execution, an off-the-shelf VLM answers one yes/no question per part and manual step, and a decoder combines the answers into a plan with an error probability per step. A dynamic program then synthesizes the task-level BT, placing inspections and operator queries at minimum expected cost under a bound on the expected number of undetected failures. Each assembly action expands into a skill-level subtree whose skills run only inside success regions measured in simulation. On 21 products with an 8B VLM, the decoder finds 42% of the annotated assembly operations, against 21% for a single plan query, and its mean error probability matches the fraction of operations it decodes wrongly. Querying the operator where the decoder is unsure reduces the expected number of undetected failures over the 21 products from 20.5 to 4.0. At the same risk, the synthesized BT costs 14–21% less than inspecting the finished product or every operation, and up to 0.7% less than simple query rules that use our error probabilities. In simulation, a Franka Panda completes an IKEA chair frame in 35 of 39 runs (a 40th run crashed the simulator) and leaves no failure undetected.

How these runs were produced. The operator is simulated: it answers queries and help requests from the benchmark's ground-truth annotations, as in the paper's evaluation, and a help request means that the operator completed that step. SUNDVIK uses the CAD models of the Manual2Skill++ benchmark. For MAMMUT and KYRRE, CAD models were derived from the IKEA-Manual meshes, scaled to the catalogue height, with 8 mm dowel joints; KYRRE's real hardware is screws, for which dowels stand in. The skill parameters (success regions, failure rates and parameter selection) were measured on SUNDVIK and are reused unchanged for the other two products.

Method

The planner never sees images or contact dynamics. It plans over an abstraction, the probabilistic task model (the assembly tree, an error probability per step, and failure and detection rates), and outputs a second abstraction, the behavior tree (BT), which a robot executes and a person reads.

  1. Probabilistic task model from graphical manuals

    Before execution, an off-the-shelf VLM answers one yes/no question per part and manual step: is this part drawn connected to other parts? The probability of Yes is read from the model's output tokens, not asked for as a stated confidence. Because subassemblies only merge, the plan is a hidden Markov chain over partitions of the parts: forward–backward decoding gives every step's posterior, and Viterbi decoding gives the most probable plan. An operation's error probability is one minus the posterior of its decoded interpretation.

  2. Task-level behavior tree synthesis

    For every assembly operation, a BT template chooses an inspection sequence (none, force, visual, or both in either order) and when to ask the operator (never, before the operation, or after a failed inspection). A failed inspection disassembles and retries the operation, and after three failed attempts the BT requests help. A subtree affects its ancestors only through the undetected failures it passes upward, so a dynamic program over these inspection contexts runs bottom-up over the assembly tree and returns the cheapest BT whose expected number of undetected failures stays within the risk bound.

  3. Skill-level subtrees with measured success regions

    The goal of each operation comes from CAD: every connector seated and the mating attachment points, such as dowel holes, coinciding. A skill's success region is the largest pose-estimate error at which it succeeds with probability at least 0.8, measured in simulation with 40 executions per radius. Backward chaining from the goal compiles the skill-level subtree of pick_up, insert and localize; if the robot's pose-error bound exceeds the success region, the subtree first localizes the parts at close range.

Results

Benchmark: the VLM-planning benchmark of Manual2Skill++, 21 products (11 IKEA furniture items, 10 toy and mechanical models) with 85 annotated steps and 66 assembly operations. The manuals are read with Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct and -32B-Instruct, used off the shelf without fine-tuning; only the decoder's prior weights, bias and temperature are fitted, each time on the other 20 products.

Q1. Reading the manual

Does the decoder recover the plan, and do its error probabilities match how often it is wrong?

With the 8B VLM, the decoder finds twice as many operations as a single plan query, and its error probabilities are calibrated.

Of the 66 annotated operations, the decoder finds 42.4%, the single plan query 21.2%, and the majority vote 30.3%. A wrong operation has a higher error probability than a correct one with probability 0.87 (AUROC, 95% interval 0.76 to 0.95), and the mean error probability, 0.61, matches the 58% of decoded operations that are wrong. With the 32B VLM, the single plan query is more accurate than the decoder (F1 55.5% against 49.6%) but overconfident: how often an operation appears in 10 sampled plans implies that 23% of its operations are wrong, whereas 38% are. The decoder implies 52%, and 51% are wrong.

Takeaway. The decoder's error probabilities are calibrated with both VLMs, and with the 8B VLM it also reads more operations.

Q2. Task-level behavior trees

Do inspections and queries reduce undetected failures, and does the dynamic program place them better than simple rules?

Querying where the decoder is unsure removes most undetected failures, and the synthesized BT costs less than fixed inspection rules.

Each BT runs in simulated executions of the task model, with 1.5 times the planner's failure probabilities and 0.8 times its reveal rates. Summed over the 21 products, executing the decoded plan without queries, as current pipelines do, leaves 20.5 expected undetected failures; our BT leaves 4.0. With its risk bound set to the risk each rule plans for, our BT costs 14.2% less than inspecting only the finished product and 21.4% less than inspecting every operation, because it queries the likely misread steps and inspects each joint before a later part covers it. Against no inspection, its cost is 6.0% higher, but the 95% interval (−20.7% to 4.8%) includes zero. Against the query rules, which use our error probabilities and prediction sets, it saves at most 0.7%.

Takeaway. Calibrated error probabilities make querying effective and carry most of the gain, and the synthesis beats fixed inspection rules.

Q3. Execution in physics simulation

Do skills with measured success regions compose into a complete assembly under physics?

In simulation, the hierarchical BT completes the chair frame in 35 of 39 runs, and no failure stays undetected.

MuJoCo with a Franka Panda, a wrist camera and a wrist force/torque sensor; the product is the SUNDVIK chair frame (four parts, six dowels). In these runs, the visual inspection checks the CAD goal conditions on the estimated part poses instead of querying the VLM. Of 40 runs from the manual to the assembled frame, one crashed the simulator. Of the other 39, 35 completed the frame, and 20 of these needed no help request. In 6 completed runs, the BT detected 7 failed attempts and redid them without help. The 4 unfinished runs stopped because the simulator cannot disassemble a fixed part. The pose-error bounds of the initial estimates (3.34 mm for a dowel, 3.77 mm for part 3) exceed the 1.5 mm success region, so the BT ran localize before every insertion.
